HTAR Liner Curing & Post-Cure Process

The HTAR liner uses a high-performance Novolac Epoxy resin system that develops its final strength, chemical resistance and temperature resistance through controlled curing and post-curing.

Heat must be applied gradually. If the liner is taken directly to the final cure temperature, the resin may soften before it has developed enough thermal stability, increasing the risk of movement, wrinkling, sagging or collapse.

The staged cure process allows the liner to build strength progressively while remaining supported by the bladder or, where the bladder has been removed, by a completely water-filled pipe. The liner must remain supported during heat-up, hold time and cool-down, and must not be drained or deflated until it has cooled below 40°C.

Support required during cure

The HTAR liner must remain supported while it is being heated, held at temperature and cooled down. Support may be provided by:

  1. Bladder — An inflated bladder during curing; or
  2. In situ — A completely water-filled pipe during post-cure where the bladder has already been removed.

Do not deflate the bladder, drain the pipe or remove support while the liner is hot. The liner must cool to below 40°C before bladder removal, draining or return to service.

Maximum cure temperature: up to 140°C

Use this method where higher cure temperatures are required and the bladder remains in place.

After the final hold, allow the liner to cool under bladder pressure. Do not deflate or remove the bladder until the liner temperature is below 40°C.

3

Unsupported Water-Filled Post-Cure

For liners already cured at ambient temperature or after bladder removal

Where the liner is already cured and the bladder has been removed, the relined pipe must be completely filled with water before heating. The water provides internal support to reduce the risk of liner softening, sagging or collapse.

The pipe must remain completely full during heat-up, hold time and cool-down. Do not drain the pipe until the liner temperature is below 40°C.

Important notes
  • Do not take the liner directly to the final cure temperature.
  • Heat must be increased in stages.
  • Hold times only start once the liner / outlet / return / far-end temperature reaches the target temperature.
  • Do not rely only on the heater or steam generator temperature.
  • Maintain bladder pressure during the full cure and cool-down cycle.
  • Steam curing above 100°C requires Head Office approval.
  • Steam curing to 140°C must be Head Office controlled or approved in writing.
  • Use only rated hoses, fittings, end caps, restraints and pressure relief equipment.
  • Record temperature, pressure and hold times for every cure.
  • Cure temperature does not automatically equal service temperature.
  • Final service temperature suitability must be confirmed by Nuflow Technical Team.
  • Where practical, cure a witness sample with the liner.
  • If pressure is lost, temperature drops, the liner moves, or the cure is interrupted, stop and contact Nuflow Technical before proceeding.

Key considerations

Choosing a resin

When choosing a resin for relining, several factors influence the work time and overall curing process:

Resin volume

The volume of resin required is directly related to the length and diameter of the liner. Larger amounts of resin generate more heat during curing, which can significantly reduce the work time. This is due to the exothermic reaction that takes place as the resin hardens. In large volumes, heat builds up quickly, accelerating the curing process and reducing the time available to properly work with the material. To manage this, it's advisable to mix resin in smaller batches or use resins designed for longer pot life in high-volume applications.

Ambient temperature

High temperatures in the surrounding environment will speed up the chemical reaction in the resin, shortening its work time. Conversely, colder temperatures can slow down the curing process. Therefore, you must adjust the type of resin and hardener based on the expected temperature at the installation site. For warmer climates, a slower-curing resin may be necessary, while faster-curing systems may be preferable in colder conditions.

Sun and shade exposure

Direct sunlight can significantly increase the ambient temperature around the liner, further accelerating the curing process and reducing the available working time. Working in shaded areas or during cooler parts of the day can help maintain a more controlled curing environment. If unavoidable, using marquees covering over the liner can mitigate this effect.

By considering these factors — resin volume, ambient temperature and sun exposure — you can choose a resin system that balances work time with optimal curing performance, ensuring a successful and durable pipe relining job.
